CISSP Domain 5.0: Identity and Access Management — Explained

Domain 5.0 is the gatekeeper domain: proving who someone is (identification & authentication), deciding what they may do (authorization), and proving what they did (accountability). Plus the access-control models, compared side by side.

Exam weight: ~13% · Free study preview from ByteBar

I&A: identification, authentication, accountability

  • Identification — claiming an identity (username). Authentication — proving it (password, token, biometric). Identification without authentication is a name tag.
  • Authentication factors — something you know (password, PIN), something you have (token, smart card, phone), something you are (biometrics). Two factors from different categories = MFA; two passwords isn't MFA.
  • Biometrics — FAR (false accept: impostor admitted — the security failure) vs. FRR (false reject: legitimate user denied — the usability failure). Tuning trades one against the other; crossover error rate (CER) compares systems.
  • Accountability — audit trails tying actions to identities: without it, authentication is theater. Clipping levels, reviewed logs, and non-repudiation make actions attributable.
  • Single sign-on — authenticate once, access many (Kerberos, SAML, OAuth/OIDC): better UX and security (one strong auth instead of password reuse everywhere) — but the SSO credential becomes the master key, so protect it accordingly.

Access control models

  • Discretionary (DAC) — owners decide (file permissions you set yourself). Flexible, and prone to over-sharing — the owner's judgment is the policy.
  • Mandatory (MAC) — the system decides via labels (clearances/classifications): users can't override even on "their" files. Bell-LaPadula territory — government rigidity.
  • Role-based (RBAC) — permissions attach to roles, users get roles. "Accountants can read the ledger" — scales beautifully, the enterprise standard.
  • Rule-based — global rules apply to all (firewall-style: "no logins after hours"). Attribute-based (ABAC) — decisions from attributes (user dept + resource classification + time + location): the most flexible, finest-grained.
  • Least privilege & separation of duties — minimum access to do the job; critical actions split across people (the person who writes checks can't sign them). Design principles, not products.
DAC = owner decides. MAC = system decides (labels). RBAC = role decides. ABAC = attributes decide. Rule-based = rules decide for everyone. Match the scenario to the decider.

Provisioning and attacks on identity

  • Lifecycle — provisioning (create with least privilege), review/recertification (does she still need it?), deprovisioning (immediate on departure — orphaned accounts are attacker starter kits).
  • Password attacks — dictionary (likely words), brute force (everything), rainbow tables (precomputed hashes — defeated by salting), credential stuffing (breach reuse), pass-the-hash (stolen hash reused directly).
  • Session attacks — hijacking (steal the session token), fixation (plant a known session ID). Defenses: secure/HttpOnly cookies, TLS everywhere, session timeouts and rotation.
  • Kerberos — tickets, not passwords, cross the network: AS/TGS issue ticket-granting and service tickets; mutual authentication; time-sensitive (clock skew breaks it). The SSO workhorse of Windows domains.

Try it: 4 sample questions

Sample questions to check your understanding of this domain. Answers and full reasoning are tucked under each one.

1. A system requires users to authenticate with a password plus a hardware token. Why is this stronger than two passwords?

  1. A. A. It isn't — any two factors are equal
  2. B. B. It uses two different factor categories (know + have) — compromising one doesn't compromise the other
  3. C. C. Hardware tokens are unhackable
  4. D. D. Passwords expire faster
Show answer & reasoning

B. MFA's strength is factor diversity: a phished password doesn't yield the token, and a stolen token doesn't reveal the password. Two passwords are one factor twice — one keylogger defeats both. Different categories, independent compromise paths.

2. A file server lets each document owner set who may read their files. An auditor flags inconsistent, over-permissive sharing. Which model is in use, and what's the structural issue?

  1. A. A. MAC — too rigid
  2. B. B. DAC — owners decide, so policy is only as disciplined as the least careful owner
  3. C. C. RBAC — roles are misconfigured
  4. D. D. ABAC — attributes are wrong
Show answer & reasoning

B. Owner-controlled sharing is discretionary (DAC): flexible by design, inconsistent by nature. The auditor's finding isn't a misconfiguration — it's the model's inherent weakness. Fixing it structurally means moving toward RBAC/MAC, not training every owner to be a security expert.

3. A biometric system is tuned so almost no legitimate users are ever rejected, but the auditor worries about impostors. Which metric captures the auditor's concern?

  1. A. A. FRR (False Rejection Rate)
  2. B. B. FAR (False Acceptance Rate) — impostors admitted; the security-side error
  3. C. C. CER (Crossover Error Rate)
  4. D. D. Throughput
Show answer & reasoning

B. FAR = the system wrongly accepts an impostor — the security failure. Tuning for convenience (low FRR) raises FAR; the auditor cares about the security side of the trade. CER (C) is for comparing systems (lower = more accurate), not for naming the concern.

4. An employee is terminated Friday at 5 PM. Their VPN account is still active Monday morning. What failed, and why does it matter?

  1. A. A. Nothing — accounts expire automatically
  2. B. B. Deprovisioning — orphaned accounts are prime attacker targets: valid credentials with no owner watching
  3. C. C. Authentication — the password was too weak
  4. D. D. Authorization — least privilege wasn't applied
Show answer & reasoning

B. The identity lifecycle requires prompt deprovisioning on departure. An orphaned account is a valid credential nobody monitors — attackers specifically hunt these. The fix is procedural: HR-triggered, same-day (or automated) deprovisioning with recertification audits catching stragglers.
