CompTIA A+ (220-1202) Domain 4.0: Operational Procedures — Explained

Domain 4.0 is the "how a professional acts" domain: safety first, document everything, follow the change process, communicate like someone who wants repeat customers. It is pure concept — no commands to memorize — which makes it perfect free-preview material.

Exam: 220-1202 · weight 21% · Free study preview from ByteBar

Safety: people and equipment

  • Electrical safety — unplug before opening; use an antistatic wrist strap clipped to the chassis; never wear jewelry working inside a PSU. Capacitors hold charge after unplugging.
  • ESD (electrostatic discharge) — the invisible killer of components. Equalize potential: wrist strap, antistatic mat, touch the chassis. Low humidity makes ESD worse.
  • Environmental — heat, dust, and humidity kill hardware slowly; surges kill it instantly (surge protector vs. UPS: a UPS also gives you ride-through time to shut down cleanly).
  • Disposal — batteries, toner, CRTs, and mercury-containing parts have regulated disposal. Wipe or destroy drives before they leave your control — data destruction is a safety issue too.
  • Cable management and airflow — blocked vents cause thermal throttling and shortened component life; tidy cabling is a reliability control, not vanity.

Documentation: the unglamorous superpower

  • What to document — inventory/asset tags, network diagrams and wiring maps, password storage (in a manager, never sticky notes), incident notes, and change history.
  • Ticketing — every issue gets a ticket: who, what, when, what you tried, what fixed it. Tickets are the organization's memory — without them, every problem is solved from scratch.
  • Knowledge base — write the fix down once so the next tech (or future you) doesn't rediscover it. The exam treats documentation as a control, not a chore.
  • Acceptable use and compliance docs — users sign them; IT enforces them. Onboarding paperwork is where least privilege starts.

Change management and communication

  • Change process — purpose of the change, scope, risk/impact analysis, backout plan, approval, maintenance window, documentation. The backout plan is decided before the change, not during the outage it causes.
  • Communicating with users — establish the problem in their words, avoid jargon, set expectations honestly ("this will take about an hour"), and confirm the fix worked for them before closing the ticket.
  • Professionalism markers to remember — be culturally sensitive, don't be judgmental about the mess you found, clarify before acting, and never take the user's keyboard without asking. The customer's data is treated as confidential, always.
  • Prioritization — impact and urgency decide order: the payroll system down for 200 people beats one person's slow printer, every time.
When two answers both look "nice," pick the one that documents, communicates, or follows the process. The exam rewards the technician who acts like part of a system, not a hero.

Try it: 4 sample questions

Sample questions to check your understanding of this domain. Answers and full reasoning are tucked under each one.

1. A technician is about to replace a motherboard. Which safety step comes first?

  1. A. A. Photograph the cable routing for reassembly
  2. B. B. Put on an antistatic wrist strap attached to the chassis
  3. C. C. Download the latest BIOS update
  4. D. D. Back up the user's files
Show answer & reasoning

B. ESD protection comes before touching anything — one static zap can kill the new board before it ever boots. The photo (A) is smart but secondary; BIOS (C) and backups (D) happen around the repair, not as the first physical-safety step.

2. A proposed firewall change has no documented way to reverse it if something breaks. What is missing?

  1. A. A. A maintenance window
  2. B. B. Stakeholder approval
  3. C. C. A backout plan
  4. D. D. A network diagram
Show answer & reasoning

C. The backout plan is the pre-decided "how we undo this" — without it, a bad change becomes an extended outage while everyone improvises. It's the change-management element the exam asks about most, precisely because skipping it is the most common real-world failure.

3. A user is frustrated and describes their problem vaguely. What is the technician's best first response?

  1. A. A. Ask them to submit a ticket and wait their turn
  2. B. B. Clarify the issue in plain language and confirm understanding before acting
  3. C. C. Take the keyboard and investigate directly
  4. D. D. Explain the likely technical cause in detail
Show answer & reasoning

B. Clarify first, act second — fixing the wrong problem wastes everyone's time and erodes trust. Plain language (no jargon), confirmed understanding, then action. Taking the keyboard (C) without asking is a professionalism violation to avoid.

4. Two tickets arrive at once: the company payroll system is down (200 users affected) and one executive's printer is jammed. Which gets priority?

  1. A. A. The printer — executives outrank systems
  2. B. B. The payroll system — impact and urgency decide priority
  3. C. C. Whichever arrived first — FIFO is the rule
  4. D. D. Both equally — split time between them
Show answer & reasoning

B. Prioritization is by impact × urgency, not by title or arrival order. A down payroll system affecting 200 people — including, presumably, the executive — dominates one jammed printer. Business-impact thinking beats hierarchy thinking.
