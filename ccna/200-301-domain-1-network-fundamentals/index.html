Cisco CCNA (200-301) Domain 1.0: Network Fundamentals — Explained

Domain 1.0 is the physical and logical foundation of the CCNA: the devices that make up a network, the topologies that connect them, the cables that carry the bits, and the wireless principles that carry them through the air.

Exam: 200-301 · weight ~20% · Free study preview from ByteBar

Network components and their jobs

  • Router — connects different networks (L3). Makes forwarding decisions from a routing table. The default gateway is just the LAN's router interface.
  • Switch — connects devices within a network (L2). Learns MAC addresses and forwards frames only to the right port.
  • Firewall — enforces security policy between zones (inside, outside, DMZ). Stateful inspection is the baseline; NGFWs add application awareness.
  • Wireless controller / access point — APs are the radios clients associate to; the controller centralizes their configuration, roaming, and RF management.
  • Endpoints and servers — the things being connected and the things serving them. The exam occasionally asks which device sits where in a small-branch design.

Topologies: shape determines fate

  • Star — everything connects to a central switch. The modern default: one cable failure affects one device; the switch is the single point of failure.
  • Mesh — every node connects to multiple others. Full mesh is expensive; partial mesh protects the critical paths. WAN and data-center cores live here.
  • Hub-and-spoke — branches (spokes) connect through HQ (hub). Simple to manage; the hub is a bottleneck and a single point of failure.
  • Physical vs. logical topology — the cables may run in a star while the traffic flow behaves like something else (e.g., VLANs creating logical separation over one physical star).
When the exam asks "which topology," it's usually asking about failure behavior: what breaks when this one link or device dies? Answer from the shape.

Cabling, fiber, and wireless principles

  • Copper: UTP categories — Cat5e (1 Gbps), Cat6 (10 Gbps to 55m), Cat6a (10 Gbps to 100m). More twists and shielding per generation. Max run: 100 meters including patch cables.
  • Fiber: single-mode vs. multimode — single-mode (long haul, laser, yellow jacket typically) vs. multimode (shorter, LED/VCSEL, orange/aqua). Fiber is immune to EMI — the reason it crosses factory floors and between buildings.
  • Wireless principles — 2.4 GHz travels farther and penetrates better but has only 3 non-overlapping channels (1, 6, 11) and heavy interference; 5 GHz is faster with more channels but shorter range; 6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E) adds clean spectrum. Higher frequency = more speed, less range. Always.
  • Virtualization in networking — virtual switches inside hypervisors, NFV (network functions like firewalls running as VMs instead of appliances), and controllers that manage many devices as one.

Try it: 4 sample questions

Sample questions to check your understanding of this domain. Answers and full reasoning are tucked under each one.

1. A new office needs 10 Gbps to desktops 80 meters from the switch. Which cabling is appropriate?

  1. A. A. Cat5e — it supports 10 Gbps at any distance
  2. B. B. Cat6a — rated for 10 Gbps at 100 meters
  3. C. C. Cat6 — rated for 10 Gbps at 100 meters
  4. D. D. Multimode fiber to each desktop
Show answer & reasoning

B. Cat6a carries 10 Gbps the full 100-meter channel; Cat6 only manages 10 Gbps to about 55 meters — and 80m exceeds that. Cat5e (A) tops out at 1 Gbps. Fiber to the desktop (D) works technically but is wildly impractical in cost.

2. Two buildings 2 km apart need a high-speed link across an industrial area with heavy electrical interference. What is the best media choice?

  1. A. A. Cat6a copper — cheap and fast enough
  2. B. B. Single-mode fiber — long distance and immune to EMI
  3. C. C. Multimode fiber — cheaper optics for 2 km
  4. D. D. A wireless bridge on 2.4 GHz
Show answer & reasoning

B. Two requirements decide it: 2 km exceeds multimode's useful range (C), and heavy EMI rules out copper (A) — fiber doesn't conduct, so interference is irrelevant. Single-mode covers kilometers easily. The 2.4 GHz bridge (D) would work but can't match fiber's speed or reliability.

3. A warehouse Wi-Fi deployment struggles with range through metal shelving. Which band reaches best, and what is the tradeoff?

  1. A. A. 5 GHz — best range and no tradeoff
  2. B. B. 2.4 GHz — better penetration and range, but slower and more interference
  3. C. C. 6 GHz — best penetration of all
  4. D. D. All bands penetrate metal equally
Show answer & reasoning

B. Lower frequency means longer wavelength means better penetration and range — 2.4 GHz wins on reach. The price: less speed and a crowded, interference-prone band (only channels 1/6/11 don't overlap). 6 GHz (C) is the worst penetrator of the three.

4. A branch office connects to HQ through a single router, and all branch traffic flows through it. Which topology is this, and what is its main weakness?

  1. A. A. Mesh — expensive to scale
  2. B. B. Hub-and-spoke — the hub is a single point of failure and bottleneck
  3. C. C. Full mesh — complex routing
  4. D. D. Star — limited to one building
Show answer & reasoning

B. Spokes (branches) through a hub (HQ) is the textbook hub-and-spoke WAN. Its weakness is structural: kill the hub (or its link) and every spoke goes dark, and all inter-branch traffic hairpins through one box. That's the tradeoff for its simplicity.
