ISC2 Certified in Cybersecurity (CC) Domain 2.0: Business Continuity, Disaster Recovery & Incident Response — Explained

Domain 2.0 is about surviving the bad day: keeping the business running through disruption (continuity), restoring technology afterward (recovery), and responding to security incidents with a practiced process.

Exam weight: ~10% · Free study preview from ByteBar

Business continuity and disaster recovery

  • BCP (business continuity plan) — keeps the business running during disruption: alternate sites, manual workarounds, who does what. Business-first.
  • DRP (disaster recovery plan) — restores the technology: systems, data, networks. IT-first. DRP supports BCP.
  • BIA (business impact analysis) — the analysis that precedes both plans: which processes are critical, what downtime costs, and therefore what RTO/RPO each system gets.
  • RTO / RPO — how fast must we recover (time) vs. how much data can we lose (point). Shorter = more expensive; the business signs off on the tradeoff.
  • Testing — plans rot. Tabletop exercises (talk through it), failover tests (actually switch), and full simulations keep the plan honest.

Recovery targets and sites

  • RTO (recovery time objective) — how long you can be down. "Email must be back within 4 hours" — RTO is a duration.
  • RPO (recovery point objective) — how much data you can afford to lose. "No more than 1 hour of transactions" — RPO is measured in time-behind, and it dictates backup frequency.
  • The pair drives design — RTO 4h/RPO 1h means: restore capability within 4 hours, backups at least hourly. Tighter objectives cost more — the BIA justifies the spend.
  • Recovery sites — hot (fully equipped, minutes to fail over — most expensive), warm (hardware ready, data/config needed — hours), cold (empty space — days, cheapest). Match the site to the RTO.
  • Backups — full (everything, slowest), incremental (changes since last backup of any kind — fast backup, slow restore), differential (changes since last full — middle ground). Test restores: an untested backup is a rumor.
RTO = how long down (time). RPO = how much data lost (time-behind). Hot/warm/cold = minutes/hours/days. The BIA sets the targets; the budget sets what's achievable.

Incident response concepts

  • Why process matters — incidents are stressful, and stressed people improvise badly. A practiced response process turns panic into procedure.
  • The lifecycle (concept level) — preparation (plans, roles, tools — decided before anything happens), detection & analysis (confirm and scope: what, where, how far), containment (stop the spread without destroying evidence), eradication (remove the cause), recovery (restore from known-good), lessons learned (blameless review, action items).
  • Key distinctions — an event is anything observable; an incident is an event with negative security impact. Triage decides which is which.
  • Roles — who declares an incident, who communicates (internally and externally), who has authority to take systems offline. Decided in the plan, not during the crisis.
  • Evidence awareness — responders preserve logs and system state for later analysis; the response team's notes become the incident record.

Try it: 4 sample questions

Sample questions to check your understanding of this domain. Answers and full reasoning are tucked under each one.

1. A company’s email system has an RTO of 4 hours and an RPO of 1 hour. What do these require?

  1. A. A. Email must never go down
  2. B. B. Restore within 4 hours of an outage, and lose no more than 1 hour of email data — which means hourly (or better) backups
  3. C. C. Backups every 4 hours, restore within 1 hour
  4. D. D. Four hours of downtime per week is acceptable
Show answer & reasoning

B. RTO is the downtime allowance (back within 4h); RPO is the data-loss allowance (≤1h behind → backups at least hourly). Swapping them (C) is the classic confusion — read which letter is which: T=time down, P=point (data) behind.

2. Before writing its disaster recovery plan, a company analyzes which business processes are critical and what downtime costs per hour. What is this analysis called?

  1. A. A. A penetration test
  2. B. B. A business impact analysis (BIA)
  3. C. C. A vulnerability scan
  4. D. D. A risk acceptance
Show answer & reasoning

B. The BIA identifies critical processes and quantifies impact — and it comes before the BCP/DRP, because the plans' RTO/RPO targets derive from it. You can't plan recovery sensibly without knowing what matters most and what it costs to lose.

3. A trading firm needs failover within minutes of a site loss, cost is secondary. Which recovery site type fits?

  1. A. A. Cold site
  2. B. B. Hot site — fully equipped and ready, failover in minutes
  3. C. C. Warm site
  4. D. D. No site — backups are enough
Show answer & reasoning

B. Hot = ready now (minutes), warm = hours, cold = days. The RTO (minutes) selects the site type; the budget just has to accept it. Backups alone (D) restore data, not a running site — recovery needs both.

4. During a suspected breach, the team isolates affected hosts before investigating the cause. Which incident response phase is this, and why this order?

  1. A. A. Eradication — remove the attacker first
  2. B. B. Containment — stop the spread first; investigating an uncontained incident lets damage continue
  3. C. C. Recovery — restore service first
  4. D. D. Lessons learned
Show answer & reasoning

B. Containment comes before deep investigation: you stop the bleeding, then diagnose. Investigating first while the attacker keeps moving is analysis during an active fire. (And containment preserves evidence — unlike wiping, which destroys it.)
