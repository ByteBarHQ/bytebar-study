AWS Certified Cloud Practitioner (CLF-C02) Domain 4.0: Billing, Pricing, and Support — Explained

Domain 4.0 is the money domain: how AWS charges, how you predict and control the bill, and what support you get at each tier. It is small, conceptual, and very testable — a great free preview.

Exam weight: 12% · Free study preview from ByteBar

Pricing models: how AWS charges

  • Pay as you go — the default: metered usage, no commitment. Most expensive per unit, most flexible.
  • Reserved Instances / Savings Plans — commit to 1–3 years of consistent usage for a steep discount (up to ~72%). For steady-state workloads you know aren't going away.
  • Spot Instances — bid on spare capacity at up to 90% off. AWS can reclaim them with 2 minutes' notice — perfect for fault-tolerant batch jobs, terrible for databases.
  • Per-service pricing quirks worth knowing — EC2 charges for running instances (stop them to stop paying); S3 charges for storage plus requests and data transfer out; data transfer in is generally free.

Tools: predict, track, and cap the bill

  • AWS Pricing Calculator — estimate costs before you build. "What will this architecture cost per month?"
  • Cost Explorer — analyze costs after the fact: visualize spend by service, region, or tag over time.
  • AWS Budgets — set thresholds and get alerted (or trigger actions) when spend or usage crosses them. Budgets don't stop spending by themselves — they notify.
  • Cost and Usage Report (CUR) — the most granular billing data, for finance teams and custom analysis.
  • Consolidated billing (Organizations) — one payer account, many linked accounts: volume discounts pool together and you see one bill.
  • Tagging — cost-allocation tags (e.g., Project: Phoenix) let you slice the bill by team, project, or environment. Untagged resources are unbillable mysteries.
Calculator = before (estimate). Cost Explorer = after (analyze). Budgets = alerts on thresholds. The exam mixes these deliberately.

Support plans: what you get at each tier

  • Basic (free) — billing support, documentation, 7 core Trusted Advisor checks, personal health dashboard.
  • Developer — business-hours email support, general guidance. For experimenting, not production.
  • Business — 24/7 phone/chat, all Trusted Advisor checks, IEM (Infrastructure Event Management) for planned events. The production-workload tier.
  • Enterprise On-Ramp / Enterprise — Technical Account Manager (TAM), Concierge team, the shortest response times. For mission-critical at scale.
  • Trusted Advisor — automated best-practice checks across cost, performance, security, fault tolerance, and service limits. Full checks require Business or Enterprise support.

Try it: 4 sample questions

Sample questions to check your understanding of this domain. Answers and full reasoning are tucked under each one.

1. A company runs steady web servers 24/7 for the next two years. How should they buy the compute to minimize cost?

  1. A. A. On-Demand — maximum flexibility
  2. B. B. Spot Instances — cheapest per hour
  3. C. C. Reserved Instances or Savings Plans — commit for a deep discount
  4. D. D. Dedicated Hosts — for compliance
Show answer & reasoning

C. Steady, predictable, long-term usage is the textbook case for a 1–3 year commitment: up to ~72% off On-Demand. Spot (B) is cheaper per hour but can be reclaimed anytime — unsuitable for steady web servers. On-Demand (A) is the most expensive option here.

2. A workload processes nightly batch jobs that can tolerate interruption. Which pricing model fits best?

  1. A. A. On-Demand
  2. B. B. Reserved Instances
  3. C. C. Spot Instances — up to 90% off spare capacity
  4. D. D. Savings Plans
Show answer & reasoning

C. Interruption-tolerant batch work is the Spot use case: massive discount on spare capacity, with the understanding AWS can reclaim instances on 2 minutes' notice. The job just retries. Committed models (B, D) waste money on bursty nightly work; On-Demand (A) overpays.

3. Finance wants an alert when the dev account exceeds $500 in a month. Which tool does this?

  1. A. A. Cost Explorer
  2. B. B. AWS Budgets with an alert threshold
  3. C. C. The Pricing Calculator
  4. D. D. Consolidated billing
Show answer & reasoning

B. Budgets watches spend (or usage) against thresholds you set and notifies — or triggers actions — when crossed. Cost Explorer (A) analyzes past spend but doesn't alert; the Calculator (C) estimates future architectures. Note: Budgets alert; they don't automatically stop the spending.

4. A team is designing a new three-tier application and needs a monthly cost estimate before requesting budget approval. Which tool?

  1. A. A. AWS Budgets
  2. B. B. Cost Explorer
  3. C. C. AWS Pricing Calculator
  4. D. D. Trusted Advisor
Show answer & reasoning

C. The Pricing Calculator estimates planned architectures — it's the "before" tool. Cost Explorer (B) needs historical data that doesn't exist yet; Budgets (A) monitor ongoing spend; Trusted Advisor (D) checks best practices, not prices. Before → Calculator.
