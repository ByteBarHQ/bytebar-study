CompTIA Network+ (N10-009) Domain 3.0: Network Operations — Explained

Domain 3.0 is about keeping the network running well: watching it, measuring it, knowing what "normal" looks like so you recognize abnormal, and planning for the day something big breaks.

Exam weight: 19% · objectives 3.1–3.3 · Free study preview from ByteBar

3.1 — Monitoring and performance metrics

You can't manage what you don't measure. Operations starts with knowing the numbers:

  • Bandwidth vs. throughput vs. goodput — bandwidth is the pipe's capacity; throughput is what you actually get; goodput is the useful data after overhead. Users experience goodput.
  • Latency — delay, usually in milliseconds. High latency kills interactive apps (VoIP, gaming, RDP) even when bandwidth is plentiful.
  • Jitter — variation in latency. Voice calls tolerate some latency but hate jitter — it makes audio choppy.
  • Utilization — what percentage of capacity is in use. Sustained high utilization means upgrade or load-balance before users notice.
  • Errors and discards — CRC errors suggest a Layer 1 problem (bad cable, bad port); discards suggest congestion.
  • Baselines — measurements of normal taken over time. Without a baseline, you can't tell whether today's numbers are a problem or just Tuesday.

3.2 — Logs, SNMP, and finding the truth

When something breaks, the logs know. Operations is largely the discipline of collecting the right data before you need it.

  • Syslog — the standard for forwarding log messages to a central server. Severity levels run 0 (Emergency) to 7 (Debug) — lower number, worse problem.
  • SNMP — polls devices for health data (MIBs/OIDs) and receives traps — alerts the device sends on its own when something notable happens.
  • NetFlow / IPFIX — records who talked to whom: source, destination, ports, byte counts. Invaluable for "the network is slow" investigations and spotting exfiltration.
  • Packet capture — the ground truth. When logs disagree, a capture at the right point shows exactly what crossed the wire.
  • SIEM — aggregates logs from everything and correlates: a failed login here plus a firewall deny there equals one story.
Order of investigation: check the cheapest evidence first — interface stats and logs — then escalate to captures. Don't start with a packet capture; start with "what do the counters say?"

3.3 — High availability and disaster recovery

Operations also owns the plan for when things fail at scale:

  • Redundancy — no single point of failure: dual power, dual uplinks, stacked switches, clustered firewalls.
  • Failover — the standby takes over automatically. Active/passive keeps one idle; active/active shares the load and both serve.
  • Load balancing — distributes traffic for performance and survives a member failing. Health checks pull dead members out of rotation.
  • Backup types — full (everything, slow, one-step restore), incremental (changes since last backup, fast backup / slow restore), differential (changes since last full, the middle ground).
  • RTO / RPO — Recovery Time Objective: how fast must we be back? Recovery Point Objective: how much data can we afford to lose? Shorter objectives cost more money — the business decides, IT implements.
  • DR sites — cold (space, no gear — cheapest, slowest), warm (gear present, data not current), hot (mirrored and ready — fastest, priciest).

Try it: 4 sample questions

Sample questions to check your understanding of this domain. Answers and full reasoning are tucked under each one.

1. Users report that voice calls are choppy but file downloads are fine. Latency averages 80ms. What should the technician investigate?

  1. A. A. Bandwidth — the link is saturated
  2. B. B. Jitter — variation in the latency
  3. C. C. DNS — name resolution is slow
  4. D. D. DHCP — addresses are expiring
Show answer & reasoning

B. Voice is sensitive to jitter (variation in delay), not just delay itself — 80ms of steady latency is fine for calls, but 80ms swinging wildly between 20 and 200 makes audio choppy. File downloads don't care about jitter at all, which matches the symptom pattern. Bandwidth (A) would hurt downloads too.

2. A core switch begins logging a rising count of CRC errors on a single uplink port. All other ports are clean. What is the most likely cause?

  1. A. A. A broadcast storm from a misconfigured VLAN
  2. B. B. A Layer 1 problem on that link — cable, SFP, or port
  3. C. C. An expired DHCP lease on connected hosts
  4. D. D. Insufficient bandwidth on the uplink
Show answer & reasoning

B. CRC errors on one port point at the physical path of that port: the cable, the transceiver, or the port hardware itself. A broadcast storm (A) would hit many ports; DHCP (C) doesn't cause CRC errors; congestion (D) causes discards, not CRC errors. Isolate to one port → think Layer 1.

3. The business requires being operational within 4 hours of a disaster and losing no more than 1 hour of data. Which objectives are these?

  1. A. A. RPO 4 hours, RTO 1 hour
  2. B. B. RTO 4 hours, RPO 1 hour
  3. C. C. MTTR 4 hours, MTBF 1 hour
  4. D. D. SLA 4 hours, SLO 1 hour
Show answer & reasoning

B. RTO (Recovery Time Objective) is about speed of recovery — 4 hours. RPO (Recovery Point Objective) is about data loss — 1 hour of transactions. The mnemonic: Time = how long you're down; Point = how far back your data goes.

4. During a "network is slow" complaint, which data source best answers "who is using all the bandwidth and talking to whom?"

  1. A. A. Syslog severity-0 messages
  2. B. B. NetFlow records
  3. C. C. The DHCP lease table
  4. D. D. SNMP community strings
Show answer & reasoning

B. NetFlow/IPFIX records conversations — source, destination, ports, byte counts — which is exactly the "who is hogging the link" question. Syslog (A) carries events, not traffic volumes; DHCP (C) only maps IPs to hosts; community strings (D) are SNMP credentials, not data.
