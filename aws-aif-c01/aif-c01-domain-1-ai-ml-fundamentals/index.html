AWS AI Practitioner (AIF-C01) Domain 1.0: Fundamentals of AI and ML — Explained

Domain 1.0 is the vocabulary of artificial intelligence: what AI, machine learning, and deep learning actually are, how models learn from data, and the lifecycle every ML project follows. No math required — just the concepts the rest of the exam assumes.

Exam weight: 20% · Free study preview from ByteBar

AI, ML, and deep learning: the nesting dolls

  • Artificial intelligence — the broadest term: any system performing tasks that normally need human intelligence (reasoning, perception, language).
  • Machine learning — AI's biggest subset: systems that learn patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules. A spam filter trained on a million emails is ML; a spam filter with hand-written keyword rules is just software.
  • Deep learning — ML's subset using neural networks with many layers. Excels at images, speech, and language — at the cost of more data and more compute.
  • Generative AI — models that create new content (text, images, code) rather than classifying or predicting. A subset of deep learning, and its own exam domain later.
AI ⊃ ML ⊃ deep learning ⊃ generative AI. If a question asks "which term," pick the most specific one that fits.

How models learn: the core vocabulary

  • Training — feeding labeled (or unlabeled) data to an algorithm so it adjusts its internal parameters. Training is expensive; inference (using the trained model) is comparatively cheap.
  • Supervised learning — learns from labeled examples: "this email IS spam." Used for classification (spam/not spam) and regression (predicting a number, like a house price).
  • Unsupervised learning — finds structure in unlabeled data: clustering customers by behavior, anomaly detection. No "right answers" provided up front.
  • Reinforcement learning — learns by trial and error against a reward signal: game-playing agents, robotics. The model explores actions and reinforces what scores well.
  • Overfitting vs. underfitting — overfitting memorizes the training data (great on training, fails on new data); underfitting never learned the pattern at all. The goal is generalization: performing on data it hasn't seen.
  • Training / validation / test split — train on one slice, tune on a second, and evaluate once on a third the model never saw during development. Testing on training data proves nothing.

The ML lifecycle and where AWS fits (conceptually)

  • Business problem → data → train → evaluate → deploy → monitor — the lifecycle to know by heart. Most real projects fail at "data," not at "model."
  • Data quality beats model cleverness — biased, incomplete, or mislabeled data produces biased, unreliable models. "Garbage in, garbage out" is an exam answer in plain clothes.
  • Deployment concepts — batch inference (predictions on a schedule, e.g., nightly churn scores) vs. real-time inference (predictions on demand, e.g., fraud checks at checkout).
  • Monitoring — models decay: the world changes and yesterday's patterns stop working (model drift). Production ML needs retraining pipelines, not one-time training.
  • Human in the loop — for high-stakes decisions (hiring, lending, medical), a human reviews or approves model outputs. The exam treats this as a responsible-AI requirement, not an optional nicety.

Try it: 4 sample questions

Sample questions to check your understanding of this domain. Answers and full reasoning are tucked under each one.

1. A bank wants a model that predicts a numeric loan-risk score from applicant data. Which ML approach fits?

  1. A. A. Supervised regression — predicting a number from labeled examples
  2. B. B. Unsupervised clustering — grouping applicants
  3. C. C. Reinforcement learning — trial and error
  4. D. D. Generative AI — creating new applicants
Show answer & reasoning

A. Predicting a number from labeled historical data is regression, a supervised task: past applicants labeled with their actual outcomes train the model. Clustering (B) groups without predicting; reinforcement (C) needs a reward loop; generative (D) creates content rather than predicting values.

2. A model scores 99% on its training data but 61% on new customer data. What happened, and what is the goal it missed?

  1. A. A. Underfitting — it never learned the pattern
  2. B. B. Overfitting — it memorized training data and failed to generalize
  3. C. C. Data drift — the customers changed overnight
  4. D. D. Label leakage — the test data was too easy
Show answer & reasoning

B. Huge training accuracy plus poor new-data accuracy is the textbook signature of overfitting: the model memorized noise in the training set instead of learning the underlying pattern. The missed goal is generalization — performing on unseen data, which is the only performance that matters.

3. A retailer wants to group shoppers by purchasing behavior without any predefined categories. Which approach fits?

  1. A. A. Supervised classification
  2. B. B. Unsupervised clustering
  3. C. C. Reinforcement learning
  4. D. D. Regression
Show answer & reasoning

B. No predefined categories and no labels means unsupervised learning; finding groups in the data is clustering. Supervised methods (A, D) need labeled examples that don't exist here; reinforcement (C) needs a reward signal, not a pile of purchase histories.

4. After deployment, a demand-forecasting model's accuracy slowly declines over six months although nothing in the code changed. What is the most likely cause?

  1. A. A. Overfitting during the original training
  2. B. B. Model drift — real-world patterns shifted away from the training data
  3. C. C. The inference hardware is too slow
  4. D. D. The training labels were mislabeled
Show answer & reasoning

B. The world moves on: seasons change, competitors act, customer habits evolve — and a model frozen at training time decays. That's model drift, and the fix is monitoring plus retraining pipelines, not a one-time training event. Slow hardware (C) affects latency, not accuracy.
